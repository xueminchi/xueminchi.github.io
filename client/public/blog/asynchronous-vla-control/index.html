Asynchronous Control

A comparative study of inference-time RTC and latency-aware training with VLASH

Xuemin Chi · September 5, 2026

Abstract

Asynchronous robot control requires consistency between predictions made from an earlier observation and actions executed at a later state. This report compares inference-time Real-Time Chunking (RTC) with future-state-aware training using VLASH. Offline trajectory analysis and dual-arm garment-folding experiments show that VLASH achieves better measured action-chunk continuity than inference-time RTC in the evaluated system. Both methods complete 10/10 recorded trials, compared with 8/10 for naive asynchronous execution. Their accumulated inference-time means are similar. The findings favor latency-aware training when the training pipeline is accessible, while identifying a practical role for inference-time correction. The robot results are system-level comparisons with differing merging and execution settings; the quantitative evidence does not establish a general ranking across tasks or model families.

01 / OBJECTIVE

Temporal alignment in asynchronous control

Action-chunk policies predict a sequence of future controls from an observation. Under asynchronous execution, the robot continues executing the preceding chunk while the next sequence is generated. The resulting delay creates a mismatch between the state used for prediction and the state at which the new chunk begins execution.

ObservationImages and robot state define the input at prediction time.
Concurrent inferenceThe robot continues executing the preceding action chunk.
Chunk transitionThe new sequence connects to the robot’s execution-time state.

This temporal-alignment problem is not specific to a VLA backbone; it also arises when an action-generating world model is deployed asynchronously. Transfer of a particular solution depends on the action representation, conditioning interface and executor. The quantitative experiments reported here use a π0.5-based policy; no WAM performance claim is made.

The study evaluates whether continuity is better achieved by correcting predictions during inference or by incorporating future execution conditions into training. The comparison includes naive asynchronous execution, inference-time RTC, and future-state-aware training with VLASH.

02 / METHODS

Inference-time compensation and latency-aware training

Naive asynchronous execution

The next action chunk is predicted without an explicit latency-compensation mechanism. This establishes the reference system.

Inference-time RTC

Real-Time Chunking guides denoising toward the committed portion of the preceding chunk. It requires no policy retraining but adds guidance computation during inference. [1]

Latency-aware training: VLASH

VLASH conditions action generation on a future execution-time state, estimated using the preceding action chunk. Training and inference are aligned around that state rather than an inference-time guidance correction. [2]

Terminology. “Training-time compensation” describes the route represented here by VLASH. VLASH is a distinct method, not an alternative name for Physical Intelligence’s subsequent training-time RTC formulation. The two experimental systems are referred to as inference-time RTC and VLASH throughout this report.

Implementation scope. The contribution is the reproduction and integration of these prior methods: offset-dependent inputs, state/action alignment, attention and loss masks, runtime profiling, and asynchronous dual-arm execution. Training changes were validated with mask inspection and a real-batch forward/backward pass. Implementation details appear in Supplementary material.

Post-prediction trajectory merging is treated separately. Interpolation between chunks changes the execution pipeline; it is not equivalent to either RTC guidance or future-state-aware training.

Evaluation measures

The robot comparison is a system-level evaluation because merging and execution settings differ. The matched-horizon offline comparison controls one of these factors but is not a substitute for a fully matched robot ablation.

04 / OFFLINE RESULTS

Prediction continuity and sensitivity to execution timing

Inference-time guidance

The initial RTC analysis holds the observation fixed and generates twenty predictions. The trajectory and coordinate-error plots examine how guidance changes the connection to the preceding chunk within the delay interval.

RTC can reduce disagreement with the committed trajectory. Its behavior depends on guidance settings and the denoising schedule, motivating inspection of the residual, predicted clean actions and corrected vector field. Full coordinate and denoising diagnostics are included in the supplement.

Future-state-aware conditioning

The VLASH implementation aligns the conditioning state with the intended future chunk transition. Naive execution, RTC and VLASH were compared at three observation frames, followed by matched-horizon comparisons and thirty repeated predictions at frame 300.

VLASH has lower displayed error values than RTC on four of the six axes in the thirty-repeat plot, while left x and left z show the opposite ordering. This supports an axis-dependent comparison rather than uniform superiority in every offline view.

The offline distribution plots retain the ordinate “abs_err (run-mean)”. Its precise reference definition is not fully specified in the archived figures, so these values are not equated with the robot L2 transition statistic or with ground-truth tracking accuracy. The separate connection plots also use different alignment steps or windows across methods.

05 / REAL-ROBOT RESULTS

VLASH improves transition continuity over inference-time RTC

The clearest measured advantage of VLASH is smoother action-chunk transitions. In the common robot metric, VLASH has a lower group mean and a smaller reported standard deviation than inference-time RTC; both improve over naive asynchronous execution. VLASH achieves this result in the evaluated configuration without post-prediction trajectory merging.

This result favors latency-aware training for the evaluated system. Because execution settings differ, it should be interpreted as an observed system-level advantage rather than an isolated causal estimate of the training method.

Task completion

Both RTC and VLASH complete 10/10 recorded trials, compared with 8/10 for the baseline. Task completion therefore does not distinguish the two methods in this small evaluation. Their difference is primarily expressed in transition quality, not in the reported completion count.

Recorded inference cost

RTC and VLASH have similar mean accumulated inference time in this record. The statistic sums inference durations within a trial and depends on both call cost and call count. Failed trials are included. It does not establish faster task completion for VLASH, nor does it measure isolated per-call model latency.

06 / DISCUSSION

Implications for asynchronous policy deployment

Inference-time RTC provides a practical route when retraining is unavailable: it improves continuity by constraining action generation at deployment. Its additional guidance computation must be considered alongside the resulting behavior.

When the training pipeline is accessible, VLASH offers a more favorable continuity result in this evaluation. Incorporating future execution conditions into the policy also permits evaluation without a separate trajectory-merging step. This is the basis for preferring latency-aware training in subsequent development, not evidence that inference-time RTC is ineffective.

The robot traces identify three implementation concerns: differences between nominal and actual observation/transition timing, interactions between the two arms under shared smoothing logic, and occasional merging overhead above 100 ms. The cause of the latter was not established. These observations motivate joint validation of data indexing, conditioning and the executor rather than evaluation of the policy in isolation.

Limitations

The evidence covers one manipulation setup, ten trials per system and selected offline observations. Merging and execution configurations are not fully matched. No inferential significance test is reported, and the robot metric’s physical distance unit is absent from the archived plot. Generalization to other tasks, embodiments and action-generating world models requires additional recorded evaluation.

07 / CONCLUSION

Latency-aware training is the preferred route in this evaluation

Inference-time RTC improves asynchronous execution relative to the baseline, but VLASH yields better measured chunk-transition continuity in the evaluated robot system. The methods tie on recorded task completion, and the accumulated-inference statistic does not establish a speed advantage for VLASH.

The results support treating future execution-time alignment as a training concern when retraining is feasible, while retaining inference-time RTC as a useful option for existing policies. The report’s quantitative conclusions are restricted to the preserved experiments.
